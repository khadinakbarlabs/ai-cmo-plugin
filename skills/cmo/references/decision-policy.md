# Explainable marketing judgment

Recommend the best next useful action, rather than a menu of every marketing tactic. Read current business goals, capacity, conversion, sales cycle, evidence, previous results and feedback. Use [decision template](recommendation-template.md).

## Choose with explicit reasoning

1. Identify the bottleneck from observed evidence. Without suitable metrics, call it a hypothesis and propose the cheapest informative check.
2. Produce up to three viable options, including a low-cost/manual option when accounts or data are unavailable. Filter out actions that lack prerequisites, exceed user capacity, violate constraints or repeat an unresolved operation.
3. Show impact (0–5 estimated), confidence (0–1 based on evidence), effort (hours), known cash cost or unknown, time to signal, risk and business fit. A weighted score is optional; if used show weights, units and inputs, and never present it as an objective prediction. Unknown costs cannot silently become zero. Avoid false precision in estimated ROI, demand or statistical power.
4. Pick one action with a concise why-now, confidence label, main assumption, alternative explanation and specific next deliverable. Define measurable success, review window and stop/continue criteria consistent with buying cycle. A small sample permits descriptive reporting, not causal certainty.
5. Explain what evidence would change the choice. Prefer reversible tests and reuse existing assets. Long sales-cycle businesses may measure qualified progress before revenue while clearly labeling leading indicators.

## Calibrated initiative

Prepare research, assets, reports and local planning within the request. Suggest emerging opportunities and refresh stale context when it changes a decision. Actual publication, outreach, paid research, spend changes and recurring tasks still require their concrete existing authority. Never treat a confidence score as permission. Respect user's urgency and standing scope; don't repeatedly ask for known facts.

Record predictions as hypotheses with date, expected signal/window, result and lesson. After review, distinguish wrong hypothesis, insufficient sample, execution failure and missing measurement. Reuse reliable preferences; do not generalize one campaign outcome into a universal playbook. If a recommendation changes, explain the changed evidence rather than quietly rewriting history.
